Should You Watch?
YES
Kaalayanam earns its 2h 42m runtime through disciplined writing and a lead performance that carries every silence. If you enjoy period dramas that trust the audience over spectacle, this is a theatre-worthy watch — not just a wait-for-OTT.
Who should watch
  • Fans of slow-burn, character-driven period dramas
  • Anyone who enjoyed the director's earlier work
  • Viewers who value performance over pace
Who may skip
  • Those expecting a high-octane action spectacle
  • Viewers with low patience for a measured first hour
  • Anyone strictly seeking light, easy entertainment

Synopsis

Set across two timelines in a coastal Telugu kingdom on the edge of colonial upheaval, Kaalayanam follows a reluctant scribe who inherits his father's unfinished ledger of debts — and discovers that some debts were never meant to be repaid in money. As he retraces the ledger's entries, the line between justice and revenge begins to blur, and a decision he must make in the present threatens to repeat the very mistake his father spent a lifetime trying to undo.

Trailer

Interesting Facts

  • The ledger prop used on screen contains over 400 hand-written entries created specifically for continuity shots.
  • The coastal village set was built from scratch over 11 weeks and left standing for local fishing communities afterward.
  • This is the composer's first period-drama score after a decade of contemporary soundtracks.
Spoiler-free review

Story

The dual-timeline structure could easily have collapsed into gimmick, but the screenplay uses it with purpose — every cut between past and present is doing thematic work, not just filling time. The central mystery of the ledger is compelling enough to carry the film's slower stretches, though a couple of mid-film detours into a subplot involving the scribe's brother feel like they belong to a different, lesser draft.

Screenplay & Direction

The director's control over tone is the film's strongest asset. Scenes are allowed to breathe without becoming indulgent, and the decision to withhold the father's motive until the final act pays off rather than feeling withheld for its own sake. A tighter edit in the second act would have made an already strong film exceptional.

Performances

The lead delivers a performance built on restraint — a raised eyebrow and a lowered voice do more work here than a monologue would in a lesser film. The supporting cast, particularly the actor playing the father in flashback, matches that register note for note. A couple of the courtroom scenes ask the ensemble to raise the temperature, and not everyone finds that gear equally convincingly.

Music & Background Score

The background score is the film's quiet triumph — it knows exactly when to disappear. The soundtrack's single folk-rooted number, used twice, earns its repetition rather than feeling recycled.

Cinematography

Shot largely on natural light with a muted, sun-bleached palette that suits the coastal setting, the frame composition favours wide, patient takes over coverage-heavy editing. A handful of night sequences lean too dark for the format, losing detail on smaller screens.

Editing & Technical Quality

At 2h 42m, the film could lose ten to twelve minutes without losing anything essential — mostly from a repetitive middle stretch. Sound design and production values are consistently strong throughout, with no jarring VFX shots to speak of; this isn't a VFX-driven film and doesn't pretend to be.

Action, Emotion & Climax

The two action sequences are brief and grounded rather than spectacular, in keeping with the film's restraint — those expecting genre-scale action set pieces should recalibrate expectations. The emotional payoff in the climax, however, lands cleanly, resisting the urge to over-explain what the audience has already understood.

Positives
  • A genuinely restrained, career-best lead performance
  • Background score that enhances rather than dictates mood
  • A dual-timeline structure that earns its complexity
  • Confident, patient direction throughout
Negatives
  • A sagging middle stretch that could lose 10–12 minutes
  • A brother subplot that feels underdeveloped
  • Some night sequences are too dark to read clearly
  • Not for viewers seeking genre-scale action
Final Verdict

Kaalayanam is a confident, disciplined period drama that trades spectacle for patience and mostly wins that trade. A trim to the second act would have made a very good film a great one — but as it stands, it's one of the most thoughtfully made big releases of the year and comfortably earns a theatre watch.

Should You Watch?
YES
Nizhal Kaalam earns its 2h 11m runtime by refusing to pad it. If procedural thrillers that trust their audience are your thing, this belongs on the big screen, not queued up for later.
Who should watch
  • Fans of tightly-plotted procedural thrillers
  • Anyone who enjoys interrogation-room tension over action set pieces
  • Viewers who appreciate editing as a storytelling tool
Who may skip
  • Those wanting a high-octane crime thriller with chases
  • Viewers seeking warmth or emotional catharsis
  • Anyone who dislikes ambiguous, unresolved side-threads

Synopsis

When a prominent businessman is found dead in his locked study, Inspector Vinod is handed a case everyone above him would rather see closed quickly. As he pulls at its loose threads, he uncovers a network of quiet complicities reaching further up the chain than he expected — and a prime suspect whose alibi is a little too clean.

Trailer

Interesting Facts

  • The interrogation room set was built with moveable walls so the camera could physically close in on characters as the tension escalated.
  • Lead actor Arjun Sarath spent two weeks shadowing a real inspector to prepare for the role.
  • The film uses almost no score in its first thirty minutes by deliberate design.
Spoiler-free review

Story

The plotting is exact rather than flashy — every scene exists to remove or add one piece of information, and the film trusts you to keep up. The mid-film reveal about the victim's business dealings recontextualises the opening twenty minutes without needing a clumsy flashback to do it.

Screenplay & Direction

Vishnu Somasundaran directs with a procedural's patience: static frames, long holds, and a refusal to cut away from discomfort. The interrogation sequences are the film's spine, and the direction never once rushes them for the sake of pacing.

Performances

Arjun Sarath underplays everything, which is exactly right for a character who has learned that showing his hand costs him leverage. Balan Nambiar, as the prime suspect, is unnervingly calm in a way that keeps you guessing until the final act.

Music & Background Score

The score all but disappears for long stretches, which is the correct choice — silence does more work here than strings would. When the theme does arrive, in the final interrogation, it lands with real weight.

Cinematography

Devika Suresh shoots interiors in tight, boxed-in compositions that make every room feel like a trap. The backwater exteriors, used sparingly, provide the only visual relief in an otherwise claustrophobic film — deliberately so.

Editing & Technical Quality

Manoj Pillai's cutting is the film's best technical asset — scenes end a beat before you expect, which keeps 2h 11m feeling considerably shorter. Nothing overstays its welcome.

Action, Emotion & Climax

There's almost no action to speak of, and the film doesn't miss it. Emotionally, it's colder than most thrillers — a deliberate choice that will land for some viewers and leave others wanting more warmth.

Positives
  • A screenplay that never once talks down to its audience
  • Editing that keeps tension high without ever feeling rushed
  • A believably morally grey suspect, not a cartoon villain
  • Sound design that uses silence as a weapon
Negatives
  • Emotionally cool — don't expect catharsis
  • The victim's sister is underused given her narrative importance
  • A slow first twenty minutes that some will find inaccessible
  • Almost no action for viewers who want genre thrills
Final Verdict

Nizhal Kaalam is a disciplined, confident procedural thriller that respects its audience's intelligence at every turn. It won't work for everyone — it's cold, patient, and light on spectacle — but for anyone who wants a crime drama built on writing rather than noise, it's close to essential.

Should You Watch?
YES
Veyyil earns its slow first half with an emotionally devastating final act. If you have the patience for a family drama that takes its time, this pays off in full.
Who should watch
  • Fans of unhurried, character-driven family dramas
  • Anyone who enjoys ensemble storytelling over a single hero arc
  • Viewers who don't mind a two-hour build-up for a big emotional payoff
Who may skip
  • Those wanting a tightly-paced, plot-driven film
  • Viewers who find slice-of-life pacing tedious
  • Anyone looking for genre thrills over quiet drama

Synopsis

In a drought-hit village outside Thanjavur, an ageing farmer tries to hold his family together as his children drift toward the city and away from the land he's spent his life working. When a medical emergency forces the whole family back under one roof, old resentments and unspoken love surface in equal measure.

Trailer

Interesting Facts

  • Several of the crop and drought shots use practical, unaltered farmland affected by a real dry season during filming.
  • The lead actor learned to operate a real well pump for his role, refusing a stunt double for the scene.
  • The film's soundtrack was recorded with live instruments only, with no synthesized elements.
Spoiler-free review

Story

It's a familiar shape — ageing parent, scattered children, a crisis that pulls them home — but the film earns familiar beats through specificity rather than shortcuts. The village setting is drawn with enough texture that it never feels generic.

Screenplay & Direction

Aravind Chezhiyan is in no hurry, and that's both the film's biggest strength and its biggest risk. The first hour meanders through daily village life in a way some will find rich and others will find slow.

Performances

Pandiarajan S. anchors the film with a performance that never reaches for sentimentality even when the material invites it. Kalyani Venkat, as the elder daughter caught between two worlds, is the film's emotional throughline.

Music & Background Score

Santhosh Narayan's score leans folk, with live instrumentation that matches the film's rural setting without ever feeling like set dressing. The recurring theme in the final act is genuinely moving.

Cinematography

Ilango Raja shoots the drought-stricken land with real texture — cracked earth, harsh light, dust — that makes the location feel lived-in rather than picturesque.

Editing & Technical Quality

The pacing is deliberately unhurried, and a tighter first hour would help newcomers to the genre stay with it, but nothing in the back half feels rushed or shortchanged.

Action, Emotion & Climax

There's no action to speak of — this is pure family drama — but the emotional peaks, especially in the last thirty minutes, are among the strongest of any film on this list.

Positives
  • A genuinely devastating, earned final act
  • Lived-in village setting with real texture
  • Understated lead performance that avoids melodrama
  • A soundtrack that never feels like set dressing
Negatives
  • A slow first hour that will test some viewers' patience
  • A couple of side characters feel underwritten
  • Occasionally repeats its central conflict without adding new information
  • Not for anyone seeking a plot-driven watch
Final Verdict

Veyyil is a patient, deeply felt family drama that asks for your time before it rewards you — but it does reward you, generously, by the end. Recommended if you're willing to meet it at its own pace.

Should You Watch?
YES
Prathidhwani remains one of the most emotionally precise dramas of recent years. If you missed it on release, it's worth seeking out on Aha immediately.
Who should watch
  • Anyone who values character-driven, emotionally honest drama
  • Fans of dual-timeline storytelling done well
  • Viewers who appreciate a story that doesn't resolve too neatly
Who may skip
  • Those seeking light, easy entertainment
  • Viewers who prefer plot-driven films over character studies
  • Anyone sensitive to themes of family estrangement

Synopsis

A successful architect returns to his hometown after years of estrangement from his father, forced back by a health scare in the family. As he confronts the reasons he left, the film moves between his childhood and present in a way that gradually recontextualises everything he believed about his parents' marriage.

Trailer

Interesting Facts

  • The film was shot entirely in sequence to help the cast track the emotional arc more naturally.
  • Director Ramesh Kondapalli based elements of the script on his own family history, though the plot itself is fictional.
  • The title track was recorded live in a single take with a 40-piece orchestra.
Spoiler-free review

Story

The dual-timeline structure recontextualises the present through the past with real precision — this isn't a twist for its own sake but a genuine act of narrative empathy. Few scenes feel wasted across the runtime.

Screenplay & Direction

Ramesh Kondapalli directs with a novelist's patience, letting silences and half-finished sentences carry as much weight as dialogue. The film trusts its audience to sit with discomfort rather than resolving it quickly.

Performances

Sricharan Kolli delivers a career-best performance built on quiet accumulation rather than big moments. Vasundhara Pantula, as the mother, is devastating in a handful of scenes that could easily have tipped into melodrama and never do.

Music & Background Score

Anirudh Bhaskar's score is restrained through most of the film, saving its emotional swell for exactly the moments that need it — the title theme, reprised in the final scene, is genuinely earned.

Cinematography

Naveen Chowdary shoots the past in warmer, softer light and the present in cooler tones, a simple device used with enough consistency that it never feels gimmicky.

Editing & Technical Quality

Ravali Marri's cut moves fluidly between timelines without ever confusing the audience about where they are, which is no small achievement given how frequently the film shifts.

Action, Emotion & Climax

There's no action in this film at all — it's pure drama — and the emotional impact is the strongest of anything on this list. The climax earns every tear it's aiming for.

Positives
  • A dual-timeline structure that's genuinely purposeful, not gimmicky
  • Two career-best central performances
  • Score that knows exactly when to swell and when to stay silent
  • An ending that resists easy resolution
Negatives
  • Emotionally heavy — not a casual watch
  • A therapist subplot feels underexplored
  • Pacing sags slightly in the film's middle third
  • Some viewers may find the ambiguity unsatisfying
Final Verdict

Prathidhwani is a quietly devastating family drama that rewards patience and holds up completely on rewatch. Two years on, it remains one of the strongest Telugu dramas of the decade so far.

Story

The premise is thin even by broad-comedy standards, mostly an excuse to string together set pieces of escalating chaos. A handful of gags land, but the plot itself is barely holding the film together.

Screenplay & Direction

Kunal Sabharwal directs at a relentless, exhausting pace that seems to mistake speed and volume for comic energy. Very little breathing room is given for jokes to actually land before the next one starts.

Performances

Rajat Mehra commits fully to the broad register the film is going for, which is admirable even when the material doesn't support it. Payal Sharma is given little to do beyond reacting to the chaos around her.

Music & Background Score

DJ Chetas Arora's soundtrack is loud and forgettable in equal measure, matching the film's general approach of mistaking volume for energy.

Cinematography

Pankaj Solanki's street photography has some genuine texture, but it's undercut by frantic, over-edited coverage that rarely lets a shot breathe.

Editing & Technical Quality

The editing is relentlessly fast without ever earning that pace — Aakash Bhatia's cut rarely lets a joke land before jumping to the next beat, which flattens most of the comedy.

Action, Emotion & Climax

There's no real emotional register here — it's pure slapstick, and even on those terms it repeats the same joke structure often enough to wear thin well before the two-hour mark.

Positives
  • A handful of individual gags do land
  • Lead actor's full commitment to the broad tone
  • Some genuine texture in the Mumbai street photography
  • Runtime is at least mercifully under 2h 10m
Negatives
  • Mistakes volume and pace for actual wit
  • Repeats the same joke structure for most of the runtime
  • Female lead given almost nothing to do
  • Exhausting, over-edited pacing throughout
Final Verdict

Shehar Ka Shor is loud where it should be sharp, and its comic ideas don't stretch nearly as far as its runtime demands. A skip in theatres — maybe a background watch once it's free to stream.
